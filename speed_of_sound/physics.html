1 · The main idea

A pulse makes a round trip

A sharp click produces a changing air pressure. Part of this disturbance enters the tube, travels to the sealed end, reflects, and returns to the microphone.

The waveform therefore contains an initial pulse and a later echo. The cursor separation, Δt, is the time taken for the round trip.

v =2LΔt

For a tube of length L, the sound travels L to the cap and L back. The total distance is therefore 2L.

Example

If L = 1.50 m and Δt = 8.75 ms, then v = 3.00 m ÷ 0.00875 s = 343 m/s.

2 · Temperature

Warm air carries sound faster

Sound speed depends on the properties of the gas. In warmer air, molecules move faster and pressure disturbances are transferred more quickly. Near ordinary room temperatures, the accepted speed can be estimated by:

vaccepted = 331.5 + 0.607T

Here, T is the air temperature in degrees Celsius and speed is in metres per second. At 20 °C this gives 343.6 m/s. The temperature should be measured near the tube, away from heaters or direct sunlight.

3 · Internal diameter

Diameter affects the shape of the echo

At sufficiently low frequencies, the pressure is almost uniform across the tube. This is called the plane-wave mode, and it behaves like a one-dimensional wave travelling along the tube.

Above the first cutoff frequency, transverse patterns can also propagate. Their pressure varies across the tube and their axial motion differs from the plane wave. Because a click contains many frequencies, these extra modes can broaden or split the recorded echo.

fc ≈1.84vπD

D is the internal diameter in metres. A larger diameter gives a lower cutoff frequency, so more of a broadband click may excite higher-order modes.

Important: diameter is not added to the distance and is not a multiplier in v = 2L/Δt. In this experiment it is mainly a check on whether the pulse is likely to remain simple.

4 · Energy losses

Why the reflection is quieter

The returning pulse is weaker because its energy spreads, some energy is absorbed by the tube and cap, and air next to the wall experiences viscous friction and heat transfer. These boundary-layer effects are relatively stronger in a narrow tube because it has more wall area compared with its air volume.

Wall losses depend on frequency, so different parts of a broadband click are attenuated by different amounts. This can change the pulse shape as well as its amplitude. The sensitivity slider enlarges the displayed waveform only; it does not recover lost information or change the measured time.

5 · Microphone position

Define the measurement reference point

If the microphone diaphragm and the click are together, a distance x outside the tube opening, the pulse travels from that position to the cap and back. The calculation becomes:

v =2(L + x)Δt

The optional microphone-offset field uses this model. Measure x parallel to the tube axis—not diagonally.

If the click is produced at the tube-mouth plane while the microphone is farther outside, the additional microphone path occurs in both arrival times and cancels from their difference. In that arrangement, leave the offset at zero. The simplest procedure is to keep the click and microphone close together and as close to the mouth as practical.

6 · A commonly confused correction

Open-end correction is not used here

In a resonance experiment, the pressure node of an open tube lies slightly outside its physical end. The extra effective length is called an end correction and depends on tube radius and the shape around the opening.

This practical measures a travelling pulse reflected from a sealed end. When L is measured to the inside face of the cap, an open-end correction must not be added. If the cap is not rigid or its inner position is uncertain, that is instead an uncertainty in L.

7 · Measurement quality

Sampling rate and uncertainty

A digital microphone records amplitude at equally spaced moments. At 96 kHz, the interval between samples is about 10.42 µs; at 48 kHz it is about 20.83 µs. A higher rate places more samples across a short pulse, but it does not guarantee that the microphone hardware actually captures ultrasonic frequencies.

The app reports the actual sample rate supplied by the browser. Cursor timing is based on sample positions, so zooming, panning, cropping and display sensitivity do not change Δt.

Important sources of uncertainty

  • deciding exactly where each pulse begins;
  • a weak or broadened reflected pulse;
  • measuring tube length and microphone position;
  • temperature varying along the tube;
  • background reflections from the room;
  • automatic processing that the browser or microphone cannot disable.

Repeat the measurement five times and use the average Δt. Consistent trials reduce random variation, although they do not remove a systematic error such as an incorrect tube length.

Further physics

The plane-wave cutoff comes from the solutions of the wave equation in a rigid circular duct. The value 1.84 is the first relevant zero associated with the radial boundary condition. Thermoviscous models add the effects of viscosity and heat flow near the walls.

For further reading, see the University of Notre Dame notes on waves in circular ducts and the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America paper on thermoviscous propagation. These optional links require internet access; the guide itself works offline.
